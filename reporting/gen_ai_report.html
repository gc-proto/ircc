Independent read

Being found is partly the problem here — more than the pilot framing implies. The prior pilots reported that AI cited at least one departmental page in 94–99% of questions. That is a looser test than this workbook applies. When we measure whether the AI cited the specific page under study, ChatGPT does so only ~36% of the time and Google ~55%. And across ~4,200 co-anchor observations, more inline citations landed on non-government sites (Reddit, Facebook, YouTube, cicnews, canadavisa) than on IRCC's own pages. Visibility at the page level is a real, measurable gap — not just accuracy.

Contractor's read

Jumping Elephants frames the result as "encouraging": judged against the full topic bundle rather than a single page, most shortfalls are structure and placement, not missing content — lighter, safer fixes than authoring. That holds in the data (only ~14% of questions are genuine content gaps). We agree on the remediation direction, but pair it with the visibility finding at left: structure fixes help the pages the AI already reaches; they do not, on their own, win back the questions where the AI never touched IRCC content at all.

The four things worth acting on

  • Accuracy is the headline weakness, and it is downstream of retrieval. Context recall is high (~0.72–0.73) — the AI retrieves the right material — but factual correctness (0.41–0.51) and faithfulness (0.37–0.43) are low. The engines have the content and still don't render it faithfully.
  • Most of the fixable gap is buried content, not absence. 137 of 388 questions (35%) have the answer on the target page but unextractable; only 53 (14%) are true content gaps. This is the case for restructuring and direct-answer leads.
  • Housing is a different, harder problem. Every "no-source" question — where the AI used zero government content — is a housing page. The engines answer from provincial, municipal and commercial sources. The lever there is upstream visibility and linking to the legitimate local authority, not rewriting.
  • The two engines behave differently. Google cites our page more often but scores lower on faithfulness and factual correctness; ChatGPT cites less but renders slightly more faithfully. Optimization has to satisfy both.

What this means for IRCC — the DG’s TLDR

Step back from the metrics. The reason this project matters is a channel shift: a growing share of people now get answers about IRCC services without ever landing on an IRCC page. The web page is becoming raw material an AI reworks, not the destination the user reads. That changes the risk profile of the whole web estate.

1 · You are losing control of the last mile. When an assistant answers, IRCC no longer controls the wording, the completeness, the currency, or the context the user sees — and you lose the analytics that tell you what people asked. Your authority is being invoked while the answer is authored by a model. Accuracy is now a channel you don’t own but are accountable for.
2 · Low faithfulness is a liability, not just a quality score. IRCC content is high-stakes: eligibility, deadlines, fees, status. Faithfulness of 0.37–0.43 means AI answers routinely drop conditions, blur exceptions, or state a rule too confidently. A newcomer acting on a subtly wrong AI answer can miss a deadline or lose status — and it will look like IRCC said it. This is reputational and real-harm exposure.
3 · The vacuum is filled by exactly the wrong sources. Where IRCC isn’t surfaced, AI grounds answers in Reddit, Facebook, YouTube and commercial immigration sites — more inline citations went to non-government sites than to IRCC’s own pages. Some are benign; some are paid consultants or look-alike domains (e.g. ircc.com, which is not a government site). Immigration audiences are among the most targeted by fraud, so an AI laundering commercial or forum content as authoritative is a misinformation and consumer-protection risk, not only a web-quality one.
4 · Resourcing has to shift from traffic to trust. Pageview and SEO metrics undercount this channel entirely. The work moves toward structuring content for machine extraction (answer-engine optimization, not just search), and toward a standing capability to monitor what AI says about IRCC over time. The cheap, high-leverage fixes (restructure, link) are real — but monitoring is a new recurring cost, not a one-off deliverable.
Bottom line for a DG

Treat AI assistants as a primary distribution channel for IRCC information that IRCC does not control. The near-term risk is accuracy (users acting on unfaithful answers); the systemic risk is displacement (the answer being built from non-authoritative, sometimes predatory sources when IRCC content is absent). Phase 1 says the fixes are mostly cheap and structural — but the strategic move is to stop treating “the website” as the deliverable and start treating “the accuracy of the answer a person receives, on any channel” as the thing IRCC owns and measures. That reframing, more than any single page fix, is what this project is really surfacing. Equity sharpens the stakes: the people most reliant on AI summaries — newcomers, temporary residents, those navigating in a second language — are also the most exposed to a wrong or fraudulent answer.

·How to read this — the method in brief

One experiment, run by the AeoGov application: put realistic questions to today's AI assistants, capture what they answered and every source they used, build an authoritative "golden" answer from the government content actually in play, then score each AI answer against that golden and compare the golden to our page.

The five moving parts
  • Queries — 388 synthetic but curated questions, one per row, written for 12 audiences.
  • Engines — ChatGPT (with web search) and Google's AI Overview, both answering live.
  • Golden answer — the ideal answer, built only from authoritative Government of Canada content the engines genuinely drew on. Where the AI used no government content, the golden is blank ("no source").
  • RAGAS scores (0–1) — factual correctness & faithfulness (the fidelity metrics), context recall & response relevancy (retrieval & on-topic-ness).
  • Gap diagnosis — for each shortfall: buried on our page (A1), on a sibling page (A2), or genuinely missing (B).
Three honesty notes carried from the source
  • Scores measure grounding, not truth. A low score means the AI answer isn't well drawn from authoritative content — not that a fact is wrong.
  • "No source" ≠ our page lacks the answer. It means the AI bypassed our pages entirely (the housing case).
  • 8 rows use page-fallback scoring (no golden existed); they are weaker and should not be pooled with golden-scored rows.

2When our pages are used, are we represented accurately?

Phase 1, question two. Four RAGAS metrics, both engines. The pattern is consistent and important: the AI retrieves the right content (high context recall) but does not render it faithfully (low faithfulness & factual correctness).

Independent read

Retrieval is not the bottleneck; fidelity is. Context recall sits at ~0.72–0.73 while faithfulness sits at ~0.37–0.43 — a ~0.30 gap. The raw material reaches the model and then degrades in the answer. Notably, citing our page barely moves accuracy: questions where ChatGPT cited the target scored 0.52 factual correctness vs 0.50 where it didn't. Being cited is necessary for attribution but not sufficient for a faithful answer.

Contractor's read & prior pilots

This mirrors JE's cross-pilot baseline (faithfulness ~0.41, factual correctness ~0.34, context recall 0.85–0.97). Their thesis: structure and JSON-LD labelling are what "move the needle," with per-page faithfulness gains of +0.17 to +0.48 in sandbox rescoring — upper-limit estimates, not production promises. Phase 2 will test whether those gains hold on IRCC pages.

·Reading the numbers honestly

  • Scores are grounding, not truth. Low factual correctness = the AI answer isn't well drawn from authoritative content, not that IRCC's facts are wrong.
  • The queries are synthetic. Curated to reflect real audiences and signals (call-centre data, top-tasks, search queries), but generated — not a live query log. Treat rates as directional.
  • Goldens are contractor-built drafts from IRCC's own content, not independently verified. IRCC's only asked task at this stage is to flag anything that looks misclassified.
  • Don't pool the 8 page-fallback rows with golden-scored rows; a missing golden often means the page itself lacked the content.
  • Sandbox uplift figures are upper limits. The +0.17 to +0.48 faithfulness gains come from favourable sandbox conditions and are the promise Phase 2 must test in reality.
  • "At least one departmental page" ≠ "this page." The program's 94–99% found-rate and this workbook's ~36–55% target-cite rate measure different things; both are true.

·Toward Phase 2 — where the evidence points

Phase 1 establishes the baseline. The data suggests four distinct workstreams for the sandbox, each matched to a gap type it can actually move.

1 · Restructure the buried pages. Direct-answer leads + query-matched headings on the 137 buried questions. Highest-volume, best-evidenced lever. Test the faithfulness uplift against JE's sandbox claim.
2 · Link the estate. 64 IRCC-owned sibling cases are internal-linking wins that help engines walk the permit journeys. Cheap, low-risk.
3 · Confirm, then author, the 53 true gaps. Only ~14% of questions. Verify each before writing — some are edge cases another owner should hold.
4 · Tackle housing visibility separately. A different, harder problem — upstream discoverability and linking to legitimate local authorities, not restructuring.

Suggested Phase 2 success test: rescore the same panel after changes and measure movement in faithfulness and factual correctness — and, distinctly, in target-page citation rate, since visibility and fidelity are separable problems.
